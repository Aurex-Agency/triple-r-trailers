6x12 vs. 7x16 Enclosed Trailer: Which Size Should You Buy?

By Triple R Trailers · September 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Find a Triple R Dealer

Choose a 6x12 enclosed trailer when your load fits comfortably and a smaller trailer suits your parking and work sites. Consider a 7x16 when you need more room for equipment, shelving, or a usable aisle. In either case, the right choice must fit your cargo, your towing setup, and the places you use it.

Do not decide from the size name alone. Interior dimensions, the rear door opening, loaded weight, and storage space can change the answer.

Triple R lists both sizes in its enclosed cargo trailer lineup. Here is how to compare them before asking a dealer for a quote.

Compare the space first

Multiplying the nominal dimensions gives 72 square feet for a 6x12 and 112 square feet for a 7x16. That is a 40-square-foot difference, or about 56% more nominal floor area.

Those figures are for comparing size labels. They are not measured usable interior space. Wall construction, wheel boxes, the nose shape, and the way a manufacturer specifies dimensions can affect the room available inside.

The additional foot of nominal width can matter as much as the additional length. It may change whether you can walk beside a machine or reach tools after loading. Measure that space rather than assuming it will be there.

When a 6x12 makes sense

A 6x12 is worth considering when you carry a defined set of tools, delivery items, or equipment and do not need a large work area inside. It can also make sense when your storage location is the limiting factor.

Picture the complete daily load. A contractor might need room for tools, material, a ladder, and shelving. A rider might need space for a machine plus riding gear, a toolbox, and securement equipment. Everything that travels needs a place in the layout.

The smaller choice works when loading and unloading remain practical. If you have to remove half the cargo to reach the one item you use at every stop, draw the layout again before buying.

When a 7x16 earns its extra space

A 7x16 is worth comparing when a 6x12 leaves too little access around your equipment or when the trailer will support a larger working setup. More floor space can accommodate a different arrangement of shelves, machines, or materials.

Avoid filling that space with assumptions. A wider trailer does not guarantee that a particular side-by-side will pass through its door. A longer trailer does not guarantee that two pieces of equipment will fit once ramps, cabinets, and tie-down access are included.

Bring the measurements of your actual equipment, including attachments and accessories. Ask the dealer to confirm the opening and interior dimensions of the exact trailer you are considering.

Also check your storage location. Measure the full trailer, including its tongue and any protruding equipment, and allow room to maneuver. A trailer that fits the cargo but cannot be stored conveniently creates a problem every time it comes home.

Make a simple floor plan before visiting the lot

Mark the proposed usable floor dimensions on a driveway or shop floor with tape. Use boxes or chalk outlines for your largest items, then add the shelves or storage you intend to install.

Walk through the loading sequence. Can you reach the side door? Is there space to secure the load? Will your most-used tool be accessible without moving a machine?

Bring this information to the dealer:

  • The length, width, height, and loaded weight of each major item.
  • The space needed to operate or open equipment doors.
  • The shelving, cabinets, spare tire, and accessories you plan to add.
  • The narrowest opening and tightest turn at your storage or work sites.
  • Your tow vehicle's exact configuration and ratings.

A short measurement exercise is easier than replacing a trailer that almost fits.

More space does not automatically mean more payload

Floor space and carrying capacity are different questions. Triple R's current category page lists two 3,500-pound axles for both its standard 6x12 and 7x16 enclosed builds. That listing does not establish either trailer's actual GVWR or available payload; ask for the labels and specifications on the quoted unit.

The basic calculation is trailer GVWR minus the trailer's actual empty weight, accounting for installed equipment. Shelves, cabinets, spare tires, and other additions consume capacity. The load must also stay within the applicable component and towing limits. NATM's explanation of trailer weight ratings.

For illustration only, a trailer rated at 7,000 pounds and weighing 2,500 pounds empty has 4,500 pounds remaining before considering other limiting ratings. Those are example numbers, not specifications for either Triple R model.

Have the dealer check the exact trailer against your vehicle's towing, payload, axle, hitch, and combined-weight limits. Include passengers and cargo in the vehicle. A “half-ton truck” description by itself is not enough to establish compatibility.

Compare door and loading arrangements

A ramp door can be useful for rolling equipment, provided the ramp's rating, width, and loading angle suit the load. Rear swing doors may suit hand-loaded cargo, but you still need room to open them where you park.

Ask for the clear opening width and height, not just the trailer's exterior dimensions. A machine's roof, mirrors, handlebars, or attachments can determine whether it fits. Low-clearance equipment may need a closer look at the ramp transition as well.

For a work trailer, check access through the side door after your shelving is in place. You should be able to retrieve frequently used items without unloading the trailer.

Compare quotes with the same features

If you are searching for a “6x12 enclosed trailer price” or “7x16 enclosed trailer for sale,” compare the build behind the number. Size is only one part of the quote.

Ask the dealer to itemize the doors, interior height, axle and brake configuration, trim, installed options, delivery, and any applicable fees. Confirm whether you are pricing a trailer in stock or a factory order. Get the current expected availability in writing.

There is no universal price difference between the two sizes. A heavily optioned smaller trailer may cost more than a larger basic build. Compare the configurations that solve your actual hauling problem.

Questions buyers ask before choosing

Will a side-by-side fit in a 6x12?

The size label cannot answer that. Check your machine's complete dimensions and weight against the trailer's clear door opening, usable interior, loading equipment, and capacity. Accessories can change the fit.

Does a 7x16 carry more weight than a 6x12?

Not necessarily. Compare the actual empty weights and ratings of both units. Extra space alone does not establish extra carrying capacity.

Should I buy bigger for future use?

Allow for realistic future needs when your vehicle, budget, storage, and access can accommodate them. If the smaller trailer already fits the work well, extra size may not help your daily routine.
