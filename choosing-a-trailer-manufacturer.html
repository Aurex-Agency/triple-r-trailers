How to Choose a Trailer Manufacturer for Your Dealership

By Triple R Trailers · September 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Check Territory Availability

A trailer line takes up more than space on your lot. It ties up cash, creates service expectations, and puts your business name behind someone else's work. Before you add a manufacturer, find out what happens between the first quote and your customer's next purchase.

The right questions cover six things: the customers you serve, the cost of getting trailers ready to sell, territory, ordering, product information, and support after the sale. A good-looking price sheet is only the start.

If you are opening a trailer business for the first time, start with our guide to becoming a trailer dealer. If you already run a lot and are comparing manufacturers, use the checklist below before placing an order.

Start with the people who already buy from you

A farm supply business, a powersports shop, and an equipment dealer may all sell trailers, but they do not need the same opening inventory.

Look at your recent customer conversations. What are people trying to move? Which requests have you turned away? Are customers buying a trailer for occasional hauling, daily business use, or one specific machine?

Mowers, supplies, and everyday haulingUtility trailersEquipment footprint, loading method, and loaded weight
Tools, deliveries, and protected storageEnclosed cargo trailersInterior space, door openings, and cargo capacity

Use this as a conversation guide, not a universal stocking formula. Your own quotes and sales should determine the mix. A manufacturer that offers several trailer categories gives you options, but the opening order should still reflect your market.

Explore the Triple R trailer lineup and identify the categories that fit the business you already have.

Compare the cost of a trailer ready to sell

When comparing wholesale trailer manufacturers, ask for an itemized quote for the same configuration. A low base price can be less useful than a clear delivered price once you account for freight, options, and preparation.

Get answers to these questions:

  • What is included in the base build, and which features cost extra?
  • How is freight calculated, and how are mixed loads handled?
  • Who unloads the delivery, and what equipment is required?
  • Are there minimum order quantities or stocking expectations?
  • When is payment due, and how are price changes handled?
  • Who handles delivery damage or missing items?

Then compare each trailer's acquisition and preparation costs with the selling price your market supports. Keep operating expenses and inventory holding costs in the picture. A quoted wholesale discount is not the same thing as profit.

Make sure the quote names the model, dimensions, axle and brake configuration, doors or ramps, options, and delivery terms. That gives you something specific to compare and something your staff can use when the trailer arrives.

Understand the territory policy in writing

Before you invest in a new line, ask the manufacturer to review your actual location. Do not assume the absence of a map pin means a territory is available.

Find out whether territory is exclusive, how boundaries are measured, whether existing dealers have different arrangements, and whether protection applies to every product category. Ask how online sales, customer inquiries from outside the area, and additional locations are handled.

The useful answer is the policy that applies to your business address and agreement. Have that conversation before you order signs or advertise a new dealership relationship.

For a Triple R location review, contact the factory through the dealer application page. Include your city, state, and a short description of your lot so the office can discuss the proposed location with you.

Learn how ordering and replenishment work

An opening load is one decision. Keeping the right trailers available is an ongoing process.

Ask how orders are acknowledged, how standard builds differ from custom orders, and how the factory communicates changes. Get current lead times for the actual configurations you want. A production estimate should have a date and a named point of contact.

Also discuss changes after an order is placed. Can you adjust a color, door, or option? At what point does the specification become final? Who approves substitutions? What happens if a customer's requested delivery date cannot be met?

Your sales team needs a dependable way to answer “When can I get it?” Keep a distinction between a trailer on your lot, a confirmed inbound order, and a build that still needs to be scheduled.

Ask for product information your staff can use

Your team should not have to guess whether a customer's equipment will fit. Ask each manufacturer for model information that goes beyond the nominal size and axle count.

For the products you plan to carry, request current dimensions, empty weights, rated capacities, door openings, loading equipment ratings, standard features, and available options. Ask how configuration changes affect those numbers.

Walk through a physical trailer with the factory representative when possible. Look at how the build is finished, how the doors operate, and how components can be serviced. Request the applicable written warranty and maintenance information before the first retail sale.

A photograph and a size label may generate interest. Accurate specifications help your staff make a recommendation they can stand behind.

Make the service conversation specific

“Factory support” should mean something your team understands when a customer calls with a problem.

Ask who receives a warranty request, what documentation is needed, how repairs are authorized, and which costs are covered under the applicable terms. Establish how replacement parts are identified and ordered. Discuss component warranties separately when they apply.

Have your own service or operations person join this conversation. The person who handles the customer's problem may ask different questions from the person placing the opening order.

Good communication after delivery matters to your reputation because customers will usually call the business that sold them the trailer first.

Find out how the manufacturer helps customers find your lot

A useful dealer relationship can include accurate directory listings, current product images, specification sheets, and a clear process for referring buyers.

Ask whether your listing can include your actual address, phone number, website, and the products you handle. Find out who updates it when your hours or contact details change. Discuss how factory inquiries from your area reach your team.

On your own website, show the trailers you actually have and use your own lot photos alongside manufacturer information. Mark sold units accurately. If a trailer must be ordered, say so and offer a clear next step for getting a current quote.

The customer should be able to move from researching a trailer to speaking with someone who knows what is available.

Bring a short checklist to the first call

Before talking to a manufacturer, write down your location, current business lines, available display space, unloading arrangements, and the trailer requests you receive most often. Have a realistic opening inventory budget and a plan for responding to inquiries.

Leave the call with these points resolved or assigned for follow-up:

  1. Whether your location can be considered for the dealer program.
  2. Which models suit your customers and what an itemized order would include.
  3. How ordering, payment, delivery, and changes work.
  4. Where your team gets current specifications, warranty details, and parts support.
  5. How customer referrals and dealer information will be maintained.
